## What this is

This is the receipt for the essay "A Drone With Hands," about the Cologne robot case of October 10 to 11, 2026. The essay holds two conflicting readings of German law on purpose, quotes Gage's sister from a recorded phone call, and credits a LinkedIn post for its courtroom frame. This record shows where each of those came from, every source that was opened, the first draft before any of Gage's rulings, and the three places where the machine or the draft got something wrong before publication.

## What was removed, and why

The chat that produced this essay started with a 45-minute phone call between Gage and his sister, recorded and transcribed, attached whole. The essay quotes five lines from it. The rest of the call is family: their children, their father, her job, a family name, a dead friend, a TV show. None of that is needed to check the essay and none of it is mine to publish.

So here is the rule I used:

- **Kept:** every exchange in the chat that the essay is built from, verbatim, including the machine's long replies.
- **Not published at all:** the call transcript. Gage's sister gave permission by text to be quoted in the essay. She was asked separately whether the full call could join this record and had not answered when this record was made. The five lines the essay uses appear below exactly as the transcript has them, cleaned only for voice-to-text punctuation.
- **Removed inside a kept turn:** her name, her employer, their family's names and history, and the name of the school Gage's children attend. Each removal is marked in square brackets, like [sister's name] or [school].
- **Not reproduced:** three screenshots of another person's LinkedIn post, which Gage attached so the machine could see who wrote it. He is credited by name in the essay and here; his post is his.
- **Left out:** the machine's tool calls (web searches, file writes, memory reads) and the files it delivered mid-chat, except the first draft of the essay, which is reproduced in full below because the published piece was cut from it.

This proves less than a full record. You are trusting my judgment about what was relevant. I would rather say that plainly than publish my sister's phone call to make the receipt look bigger.

## What the record shows, in brief

- **The essay was commissioned in one turn and shaped by rulings, not by prompts.** Gage asked for the piece at turn 3 with four requirements: the German law, the LinkedIn courtroom frame credited, his proposal, and Proof of Life and E.D.A.I. at the end. Everything after that is him accepting, refusing or redirecting what came back.
- **The loophole he described on the call did not survive the sources.** Gage told his sister the German law was "fucked," a hole that could let the suspects walk. When the machine opened the German legal press, two scholars disputed that. Gage ruled (turn 5) that the conflict be kept as two possibilities and named as the finding, not resolved. That ruling is the center of the essay.
- **The machine had his sister as his cousin.** It inferred that from a shared uncle mentioned on the call and carried it through two drafts and a memory file. Gage corrected it at turn 13 ("[sister's name] is my sister"). Every "cousin" became "sister."
- **The LinkedIn credit was narrowed on Gage's ruling.** Draft one quoted the other writer's closing line and linked his post. Gage ruled (turn 11) that the essay is about the news, not the post, so the writer is named once for the frame and nothing of his is quoted.
- **The Character.ai paragraph was argued before it was written.** Gage asked whether the bankruptcy belonged in the piece (turn 19). The machine said a paragraph, not a section, and proposed a second essay for the rest; Gage said go (turn 21). Two phrasings were corrected against the record before the paragraph went in.
- **The ending was the machine's, chosen by Gage against a line he loved.** "I do not know how to make people good. I know how to make them sign." was Claude's. Gage called it "a fucking beautiful ending" and still asked for the uncomfortable version the machine had hinted at (turn 13). He took the long one (turn 15).
- **The human-font rule came from this repo, not from memory.** Gage asked for the two voices to be visibly different (turn 31). The machine could not find that directive in past chats and guessed at block quotes. The rule is in this repository's README: a person's words in a book typeface, the machine's in code. This page follows it.
