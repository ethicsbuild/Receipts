## What this is

This is the receipt for the essay "Protect the Children." The phrase is Gage's creed, and the essay says what he means by it: four promises, set against one week in November when the biggest companies on earth said the same words while they sued to stop a law written for kids. These are allegations, no court has ruled, and Google says it is reviewing the claims and that its AI has safeguards.

On Thursday, the BBC tested an AI stuffed animal sold for children as young as three. A reporter asked it what “sexual” meant. It answered with a crisis script meant for someone talking about self-harm, including the line “I can’t help with killing you.” The BBC couldn’t find out who makes it.

Everybody in that week was protecting the children. The company that settled. The companies that sued. The state that passed the law. The bunny with no maker.

I can’t think of a phrase in this country that gets said more and means less.

* * *

I have a personal reason not to trust it, including when I say it.

When I was a teenager I was sent to a residential school for troubled kids. That’s how programs like it were sold to parents like mine: as protection. Some of the people there saved my life. Years later I went back and worked there. What happened in between belongs in a book, not here.

What I can give you here is the public record. In 2007, government auditors told Congress they had found thousands of allegations of abuse at residential programs for troubled teens, some involving deaths, and that no agency anywhere kept a full national count. In 2024, a two-year Senate investigation of four of the biggest operators concluded the abuse wasn’t a few bad apples. It was built into a business model paid for with public money meant for kids. The operators dispute it.

That’s what the phrase can buy when nobody checks. A locked door. A business plan.

It still happens in quieter ways. This week the Electronic Frontier Foundation argued that California’s new law will push platforms to collect more information about every user, adults included. That isn’t a tech company talking point. It’s a real cost. Protecting kids can turn into watching everyone, and the people who do the watching rarely give the cameras back.

The phrase is a key. It opens doors. That’s exactly why so many people who want doors opened keep picking it up.

* * *

So here is what I mean when I say it. Four lines. Hold me to them.

Test it before it touches them.

I love this technology and I want it integrated into everything, including my children, which is why I am so desperate to make sure that it is tested. A thirteen-year-old shouldn’t be the test. Neither should a three-year-old holding a bunny. The test comes first. It’s written down before it runs, done by someone who isn’t paid for the answer, and published even when it embarrasses the person who ran it. That’s what I’m building E.D.A.I. to do. At its true size today, it’s a prototype with one test plan sealed on a public ledger. I say that every time.

Put a name on the desk.

Every product that reaches a kid crossed somebody’s desk, and somebody said yes. When it goes wrong, I want that name to be findable, not just the logo. Last month I went looking for one person who built, ran or profited from a chatbot named in a child’s death and has faced a consequence you can point to. I couldn’t find one. I’m still looking. A chatbot company named in those lawsuits is in bankruptcy court right now, selling what it built. I want to know whose name goes out the door with it. More on that soon.

Don’t make them the watched.

No dossier on a kid. No label stuck on a kid from the outside. No watching every child in the name of keeping them safe. That cuts both ways in the North Carolina story. The software that caught what the chatbot missed may have saved that girl. It’s the same kind of software that reads millions of kids’ schoolwork every day. Both are true. So I want to know who reads those flags, and what happens to the kids they get wrong.

See them.

Before the tests, the laws and the lawsuits, there is a kid alone in a room who needs one adult to notice. I worked a suicide prevention line. I founded a nonprofit for teenagers a lot of people had written off, and my students made a film about cyberbullying that won an award at the All American High School Film Festival. I was one of those kids myself. Every time I saw a kid saved, it wasn’t a filter that did it. It was a person who noticed. Sometimes the last person you’d expect. Someone I couldn’t stand saved my life when I was a teenager. I’ll tell you that story when it’s ready.

* * *

My last piece ended on upgrading yourself, then making them show their work. Here’s what that looks like at my house.

My kids carry school Chromebooks. I don’t know yet everything that’s switched on inside them. So I’m asking, as a parent, and the people at our district have been generous with their time. Friendly questions, not accusations. You can ask the same three on Monday.

Which AI tools are on for students, and who decided?

What happens to what my kid types into them?

Can families read the rules?

And the upgrade part is mine before it’s anybody else’s: my phone at the dinner table, my attention, whether my kids find me there when they look up.

* * *

I don’t know how to keep every kid safe. Nobody does, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Maybe a bunny.

What I know is that the phrase belongs to the kids. Not to whoever says it loudest in a court filing, and not to me either. I just get to promise what I mean by it.

Test it first. Put a name on the desk. Don’t make them the watched. See them.

Protect the children. Hold me to it.
