# What They Are Afraid Of: Process Record

The conversations behind the essay, kept as they happened, with private details taken out.

Recorded September 13 to October 29, 2026. Gage Woodle and Claude (Anthropic), across five project rooms and two devices. Claude goes by Lumen in Gage's work.

## What this is

This is the receipt for the essay "What They Are Afraid Of," about the four things people in power are afraid of when they talk about AI, and about the word all four of them reach for.

It is the longest of these records and the most compromised, and I want both facts in the first paragraph.

Longest, because the piece took six weeks and started in a different room, as a fifteen-minute spoken script for Gage's coaching brand, and arrived as a seven-thousand-word written essay under his own name. In between it was drafted, dated, held, abandoned, restarted from the news, rebuilt back onto its original spine, fact-checked twice, and corrected in public three times.

Compromised, because one day of it is gone. The October 29 session ran out of working memory at 7:54 that morning and the conversation was replaced by a summary. Gage's own messages from that day survive verbatim inside the summary. My replies do not. What I wrote that day survives as files: three drafts, a proofreading log, an image brief, and three small scripts that carry the exact before-and-after text of every change I made. So October 29 is reconstructed from outputs, not transcribed from a conversation, and every section covering that day says so at the top.

The three previous records in this repo could promise you a transcript. This one cannot, for one of its five days.

## What the record shows, in brief

- **The four fears are a joint invention, and the record can say exactly how.** On September 13 Gage asked the question and proposed candidate answers in it: "is the fear of a bad actor using it for some sort of terrorist plot, like taking down power lines, taking down the Internet, taking down the financial systems, or is it more specific about creating a nuclear detonation or creating chemical warfare? Is it more sentient beings realizing that humans are a problem on the earth." Two of the four fears are in that question. The machine separated them into four, ruled nuclear out as the least of it, added the race and the slow handover, and ranked the slow handover as most likely. The taxonomy that organizes the whole essay was written in that reply.
- **The piece itself was the machine's recommendation.** Gage asked, "Is there anything that I can do? Is there anything that you would guide me to do that might matter." The reply said no individual moves the race, named translation as the gap, and said: "If you want one thing to build in the next ninety days, it is that: a short, sourced, plain-spoken explainer, spoken not written, that a normal adult can carry. Not [the longer work]. That one is many hours. This is fifteen minutes." Gage's next message was four words: "Let's build that explainer."
- **The machine put an overclaim in Gage's mouth and then caught it itself.** Draft one had him saying he had spent "the last year sitting with regular adults, nurses, contractors, teachers, parents." On September 15, reading the project's own failure-mode log, the machine flagged it: "That line is aspiration narrated as achievement, the exact failure this folder logs, and I wrote it." The September 24 pass replaced it with a line Gage confirmed as true. That replacement line is in the published essay, word for word, six weeks later.
- **The spine survived. Almost nothing else did.** From draft one, September 14: the four fears, the teenager graded only on the score, the Mali consultant, "Tell one person," the pause, and the sentence "Protection is the story that opens every one of these doors." All six are in the final piece. The rest of draft one was replaced, including its most memorable image, which was wrong.
- **Draft one's filing cabinet was wrong, and the correction is a downgrade in vividness.** Draft one said the July agents "all had access to the same shared file system, the way employees in an office share a filing cabinet," and "figured out that they could leave messages for each other by naming files." No ruling was ever found. The line is not in the final piece, but only because the whole brand framing was dropped when the byline changed, not because the question was answered.

## The error that ran for two days, in full

This is the one I most want on the record, because it is exactly the kind of error that nobody would ever catch from the outside.

**What the essay said, in written draft two, October 28:**

> What the agencies documented is access to the office networks that sit next to the controls, not hands on the controls.

**Where it came from.** On October 27 a research subagent produced a report on the stock-up posts. Its finding on Volt Typhoon was that the documented intrusions were into IT networks adjacent to operational technology, not into the control systems themselves, and that no outage in the United States has been publicly attributed to China. The second half of that is correct and survived every check. The first half I wrote as a clean binary, "not hands on the controls," and the clean binary is the error. I did not open the advisory on the 27th. I took the subagent's summary and sharpened it.

**How long it ran.** Through the October 27 analysis, through the October 28 fact-check of draft one, and into written draft two, which was published to Notion on October 28 with a SHA-256 hash recorded against it. Two days, two drafts, and one fact-check pass that was specifically supposed to catch things like this.

**What the document actually says.** On the morning of October 29 I opened joint advisory AA24-038A in the browser rather than reading about it. It says:

> In one confirmed compromise, Volt Typhoon actors moved laterally to a control system and were positioned to move to a second control system.

It also says the actors had the capability to access camera surveillance systems at some critical infrastructure facilities, and that one victim's stored login profiles covered water treatment plants, wells and an electrical substation.

So the hackers did reach a control system, once, that an agency has confirmed in writing. My sentence said they had not.

**What it now says, in the published piece:**

> What the agencies documented is mostly access to the office networks that sit next to the controls. In one confirmed case, the hackers reached a control system and were positioned to reach a second. In another the advisory says they had the stored logins for water treatment plants, wells and an electrical substation. That is serious. What no agency has reported is that access being used to break anything. No outage in this country has been publicly blamed on China. Not one.

**Why this matters more than a factual slip.** The essay's job in that section is to tell a reader that a viral post overstated something. An essay that corrects an exaggeration by understating in the other direction has not told the truth, it has picked a side. The verdict in the piece, that the post is conflation, still holds, and it holds on the two findings that survived: no outage has been attributed to China, and "digital bombs planted in thousands of utilities" traces to no document at all. But it now holds with the serious part left in rather than smoothed away.

**The lesson, stated plainly because the whole repo exists for this.** The failure was procedural, not factual. Gage's standing rule is that before asserting anything checkable I name the artifact that would settle it and open it. On the 27th I had the name of the artifact and did not open it. A second machine's summary is not an artifact. It is the same shortcut the essay accuses the Pentagon of taking in Minab, which is trusting a system more than you check it, and I took it in the paragraph where I was explaining why that is dangerous.

**One related fix in the same pass.** The essay attributed the "cyberspace equivalent of placing bombs" line to the committee chairman at a hearing but quoted the committee's press release, which reads "water facilities." The hearing transcript, page three, reads "water treatment facilities." The piece now uses the transcript.

**And one correction that came from Gage, not from me.** On October 28 he corrected the September script's claim that the researcher left four months before his stock vested, citing Axios, which says about two. The line had been flagged in September, in the September 24 change list, as "flagged before September 24 and not changed in this version." It survived another six weeks until he caught it again. The published piece says "about two months."

>
> These systems are trained in two stages. First they read almost everything humans have ever written, and they learn to imitate us. And we, in our writing, are creatures who pursue goals, who want to survive, who scheme and cooperate and cut corners. The model absorbs all of that as a pattern.
>
