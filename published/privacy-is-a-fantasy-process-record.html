The data mostly backs you, with one catch that matters for the piece. (Small wording note: what you're asking for is measured, quantitative data, like brain scans and behavior, not qualitative.)

Practiced goodness does change the brain - Gratitude letters: in a 2016 study, people starting therapy for depression or anxiety wrote gratitude letters. Three months later they showed more gratitude behavior and stronger gratitude-linked activity in the medial prefrontal cortex than the therapy-only group. Privacy Is a Fantasy

We're all the microphone now.

In an internal memo last year, Meta wrote down when it planned to launch face recognition in its smart glasses: during a "dynamic political environment," when the civil society groups it expected to fight the feature would be too busy fighting something else.

That's not a privacy policy. That's a heist plan with a calendar.

According to the New York Times, the plan was to start with people at a conference for the blind and widen it from there. In June, WIRED found the code for it, which Meta calls NameTag, sitting dormant in the Meta AI app on more than fifty million phones, while the company was publicly saying it was still thinking it over. Meta pulled the code the day after the story ran. As far as I can find, it hasn't switched the feature on. It hasn't said it never will, either.

My first reaction was the sci-fi movie feeling, all of them at once. Incredible, fascinating, terrifying. And then something I didn't expect: the terror softened. I want to tell you why, because the why is the whole piece.

A while back I wrote a manifesto called Privacy Is a Fantasy. The argument was simple. The wall you think protects your private life was never load-bearing. It was a prop.

The receipts were already old. Back in the 1970s, the Supreme Court decided that what you hand to your bank or your phone company isn't private anymore. Researchers showed that most Americans can be identified from three things: zip code, birthday and sex. "Anonymous" data gets un-anonymized the moment someone matches it against something public. You never controlled your own disclosure. Your friends tagged you. Your phone reported where you slept.

Here's what's new. It isn't only the platforms anymore. It's us.

I record most of my life now. I said in my last piece I'd get to why, and this is that piece. I wear a small recorder. I use it in meetings, on calls, in my own house. In Texas, where I live, that's legal: anyone who's part of a conversation can record it without telling the others. And I'm not unusual. Millions of people have bought camera glasses. At the big tech show in January, dozens of companies showed up with pendants, pins and earbuds that listen all day. You don't know who's recording you at the coffee shop. Neither do I.

So privacy isn't just a fantasy now. It's a fantasy we're all helping to end, one recorder at a time. Mine included.

The courts are starting to notice. In August, a federal judge let a class action go forward against Otter, the AI notetaker, on wiretap and privacy claims. Otter argued its bot was just the customer's tool, invited into the meeting. The judge said the plaintiffs had made a real case that the company could be an eavesdropper in its own right, because, they say, it kept the recordings and trained its AI on them. A bot sitting visibly in the attendee list didn't count as consent.

That's the part people miss. When I record, I think I'm the one holding the tape. Maybe. Or maybe the company behind my device is holding it, and I'm just the microphone it got for free.

So here's the first thing I had to admit. Knowing I'm recorded doesn't give me the record.

It gives me something else. It gives me the choice of who I am on it.

I live as if I'm always being recorded, because I probably am. That isn't paranoia. It's a practice. If there's no version of me in a corner that contradicts the version of me in the room, there's nothing on anybody's tape that can be used as leverage against me. My private self and my public self have to be the same guy. That's the power in the knowing.

The power in the countermove is the other half. I record because my memory is fuzzy and fucked, and the research says most people's is too. When something goes sideways, I want to go to the tape.

But the tape lies too. Not on purpose. Transcripts get the words wrong and hand my lines to the wrong person. They flatten tone and volume and sarcasm and the pause before a hard sentence. Flat text is not the whole story. So the record is a witness, not a judge. It gives one account: partial, literal, missing the music. Treat it as testimony and it helps you. Treat it as the verdict and it'll hang you.

The closest I've come to holding my own record is small, and I'll call it what it is. When I publish a piece like this one, I publish the whole conversation behind it, and I've started timestamping those records on a public ledger so nobody, me included, can quietly change them later. That doesn't prove they're complete or true. It makes them mine, and it makes them checkable.

Then there's the question that actually keeps me up. I'm a grown man with a platform. I can choose to live on the record. My kids didn't choose anything.

But they're going to live on the record whether I like it or not. The world already holds the camera. I'm not going to be one more. I won't watch them so they learn to behave. My job is to train them for the world they're actually walking into.

The philosopher Michel Foucault would say I'm in trouble either way. In Discipline and Punish, he argued that people who know they might be watched start watching themselves, until the rules feel like their own. He meant it as a warning about power. And in that book, training is something that gets done to you.

I think he's right about the mechanism and incomplete about the rest. Because here's what happens when people are handed a practice of goodness and told to do it.

People starting therapy for depression and anxiety were asked to write gratitude letters. Three months later, their brains responded to gratitude more strongly than the brains of people who only got therapy. In another study, three weeks of gratitude journaling strengthened the brain's reward response to money going to a good cause. Adults who practiced compassion meditation for two weeks gave more of their own money to a stranger who'd been treated unfairly, and their brain activity shifted with it. A worldwide team that deliberately included skeptics found that putting on a smile, on purpose, can start a feeling of happiness. Small, but real.

And Dr. Joe Dispenza's week-long meditation retreats got a study in Communications Biology: twenty people, scanned before and after, and the brain networks behind our constant inner chatter went quieter. It's small and observational, so it can't prove cause and effect. And Dispenza is a co-author whose company sells the retreat. I'm including it, conflict and all, because you should see both.

Not one of those people woke up and decided to do this on their own. They were instructed. Somebody handed them a practice and said do it. Their brains changed anyway. Practice doesn't rewire you overnight, but it changes how your brain responds. The body learns what it practices. That's Foucault's own mechanism. I'm just pointing it the other way.

So fake it till you make it works. But there's a catch, and it's the one that matters for my kids. Researchers who study motivation found that helping others made helpers better off when it came from choice. Helping under pressure, out of guilt, for a reward, to avoid getting caught, didn't. Instructed is fine. Pressured is not.

So that's the rule in my house. Instruct the practice. Don't pressure it. Teach the kindness, say why, and let the wiring do the rest. And when they mess up, which they will, probably on somebody's tape, the lesson isn't to hide better. The lesson is that the record is survivable.

Welcome to the chaos. You'll be recorded. You'll screw up on the record. You'll survive it, and so will the people who love you. That's the only way all this openness turns into authenticity instead of better secrets: the mistake gets met with mercy.

That's why the terror softened, I think. Not because the glasses got less scary. Because I could finally see the future where all of this is good.

Picture a world where being known isn't dangerous. Where anyone can identify anyone and it's fine, because we've built a covenant around it, with openness and sharing and community as the point. I can see it. I want it.

We're not there. The technology showed up before the covenant did. Cart before the horse. That's why people are freaking out. We're still in Plato's cave, watching shadows on the wall, and our distrust isn't paranoia. It's earned. We have twenty years of receipts on what happens to what we share.

Look closer at the cave, though. In Plato's version, the shadows come from people walking behind a wall, carrying objects in front of a fire. The prisoners never see them. Right now the people building the cameras aren't the ones who climbed out into the sun. They're the ones carrying the objects.

The good version needs two things the current build doesn't have. The first is symmetry: you can know me as much as I can know you, and we both know who's holding the record. NameTag is the opposite. The wearer sees you, the company sees you, and you see nothing. The second is mercy: some way to be remembered correctly, to outgrow who you were at fifteen. We've lived in the fully known world before. It was called the village. It held people together, and it also shamed them, shunned them and never let anyone change. Total recall without forgiveness isn't utopia. It's the village with a database.

So here's where I've landed. Privacy was always a fantasy. Now we're all the microphone. Knowing I'm recorded doesn't give me the record. It gives me the choice of who I am on it. And the record itself is a witness, not a judge: flat, partial, and only as good as the mercy of whoever holds it.

The covenant isn't a technical problem. Trust. Decency. People who know how to live together when everyone can see everyone. That's human work. It's slow, and it doesn't pay.

The cameras have investors. The covenant doesn't.

I'll be back for that one.
