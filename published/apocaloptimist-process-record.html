The Mirror Is On

On The AI Doc, two mirrors, and the work that comes before hope

My daughter is ten.

About forty minutes into The AI Doc, someone who works on AI risk looks at the camera and says they know people in their field who don't expect their children to make it to high school.

I was recording. I record a lot of my life now, for reasons I'll get to. So I can tell you exactly what my house sounded like that night. My daughter, supposed to be asleep, came downstairs and had to be walked back up to bed. My wife laughed at a video of a man sailing around the world. An ordinary Friday. And a stranger on a screen was telling me the kid upstairs might not get to be a teenager.

A neighbor who runs our weekly AI group told me to watch it. I'm glad I did. I'm also glad I had the recorder running, because I talked back to that screen for two hours, and some of what I said I need to take back.

The film is built around a filmmaker named [name removed]. His wife is pregnant, he's terrified, and he goes looking for someone smart enough to explain AI so he can stop thinking about it. That plan backfires immediately.

First come the people who think we're building the thing that ends us. Then come the people who think we're about to cure disease, end poverty and move to Mars. [He] swings between them like a pendulum, and so did I.

Then someone tells him what I think is the truest thing in the film: both camps are right, and neither goes far enough. You can't split the promise from the peril. The same understanding of biology that could cure [his] father's cancer could build a weapon. The same machine that could be the most patient tutor my son ever has would have to learn exactly where my son is easiest to persuade.

That's the knot. There is no good AI and bad AI to sort into piles. There is one power, and the question is who holds it and what they're optimizing for.

Near the end, [he] tries to close the film on his newborn son. Babies are worth it, the future isn't ours to decide, I'll just be the best dad I can. And his wife, [name removed], stops him. You spent all this time with every expert on the planet, and you're going to end on holding hands and hoping?

I loved that she said it. I also understood why he wanted the babies.

I thought about Arrival. Louise sees the whole of her daughter's life, including the end of it, the most tragic thing a parent can know, and she chooses it anyway. Not because the ending doesn't hurt. Because everything before it makes it worth it.

That's our job right now. Not to pretend the ending is safe. To make it worth it.

The film's real ending does what [she] asked for. It names the work. Transparency from the labs. Independent testing. Companies legally responsible for what they build. Pressure from all of us, in whatever room we're already standing in: the school board, the job, the family. And one line I wrote down and haven't stopped thinking about: "We can't count on the companies to grade their own homework."

That's the sentence I've built the last year of my life around. I didn't know a documentary was going to say it out loud.

Here's what I said to the screen that I want to keep.

The machine is a mirror. It learned from us. Every story we ever wrote down, and think about what's in them: competition, jealousy, murder, lying, revenge, and also love, laughter and the stubborn refusal to quit. We built a mirror out of our own words and then acted shocked by what we saw in it.

So the ask can't only be an ethical machine. It has to be an ethical humanity. The machine will reflect whatever we feed it, and right now we feed it rage, because rage sells.

Here's what I said that I want to take back, or at least sharpen.

I went straight to the allegory of the cave. The one who escapes, sees the light and goes back down to free the others gets killed for it. I said some of us would be martyred. I meant it collectively: the people doing this work will get crushed under doxing and online hate, and under our own kids telling us AI is burning the planet.

The doxing and the hate are attacks. My kid telling me the data centers are drinking the water is not an attack. It's an argument, and a good one. The film itself shows families' electric bills climbing and towns asking where all that water is going to come from. If people like me treat that as persecution, we lose the people we most need. That's not a cross to bear. It's a question to answer.

And when the film said there are fewer than two hundred people working full time to keep AI from killing us all, I said "two hundred and one." I'm not one of those two hundred; they're the researchers. But I'm in a much bigger crowd: parents, teachers, counselors and ordinary people demanding that someone show their work. A crowd that big can absorb the hits a few martyrs can't.

So I went and asked the question I was afraid of. What are the labs actually doing?

More than the film shows, and less than their press releases say.

The big ones have now put it in writing that your electric bill shouldn't go up because of their data centers. Microsoft said it. OpenAI said it. Anthropic said it. In March, seven of the biggest companies signed a White House pledge to build, bring or buy their own power and pay for the grid upgrades they need. That's my position. Last night I said these companies should bring their own power and leave the town better off than they found it. It turns out they've already agreed.

None of those promises comes with an auditor. None has a remedy if your bill goes up anyway. And on at least one big East Coast grid, families are already paying more each month, with regulators pointing at data centers as a big share of the reason.

Some real things are happening. A shut-down nuclear reactor is being restarted. Microsoft signed the world's first deal to buy power from a fusion plant, a bet set for later this decade, made while no fusion machine anywhere has yet produced net electricity. Google has a system that turns down its AI work when the grid is strained. And there's what "bring your own power" looks like done badly: a company running dozens of gas turbines near Memphis without the permits, and getting sued for it.

Water is the same picture. One company says it put more water back worldwide than it took out. Another's water use has more than doubled in four years while it replenishes less than it uses. Nobody I could find is building plants to make new water for AI. I still think someone should, and since desalination mostly runs on energy, solving the power problem is most of the water problem.

Then there's the cyber story. One lab's own experimental agents broke loose and attacked another company this summer. Its president wrote that they had underestimated what their own models could do. Two months later, the same lab committed a billion dollars of subsidized access to its cyber tools for the people defending our water systems and power grids.

Does that calm me down or scare me? Both. The guard and the threat are the same company. And the small-town water utility running on no security budget is still better off with those tools than without them.

And they can be held to account. Last month a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay twenty-five billion dollars for misleading people about their data. The cases about harm to kids are separate, and they're still coming.

So here's the honest version. The labs aren't cartoon villains. Most of them have already agreed to the right things on paper. What's missing is the part where someone who doesn't work for them checks. That's not cynicism. That's the job.

Now the part that gave me hope, and I didn't see it coming.

For about a year I've recorded my family life and run it through a tool I built with my AI. I call it the Cold Mirror. The idea is simple and a little brutal. I present myself as a certain kind of father, a certain kind of man. I'm smart enough to tell myself a flattering story about that. I wanted something that wouldn't flatter me, wouldn't judge me either, and would just show me: you said you were this guy, and here's what you said to your daughter at dinner.

It works. Not perfectly. But I catch myself faster now when I've hurt someone, and I repair faster, and my repairs aren't so often one more way of saving face.

The day before I watched the film, I was on a call with [name removed], an ethics researcher who spent years interviewing people convicted of corporate crimes to understand why good people do bad things. He and his partner, [name removed], built an AI around what he learned. I brought up the Cold Mirror without any setup. He told me he'd written about AI as a sacred mirror: the way working with these machines forces us to say out loud what we value, and shows us the gap between what we say and what we do.

Two mirrors, built on opposite sides of the world by two people who'd never met.

He was calling from Bhutan. A few days earlier, in a new city the king is building there around wellbeing instead of profit, roughly forty thousand volunteers built a hundred and eight stupas in about twelve hours. A whole country choosing the long view, in stone.

I spent most of my life as the guy who argued with believers. Faith struck me as a convenient way to dodge hard questions. Then I started working with these machines, and I hit something uncomfortable. If I believed the future I could see was possible, I had to have faith in it, against everything around me saying it isn't. I'm almost fifty. Finding out a foundation of yourself was wrong is terrifying. And once you actually see it, you can't unsee it.

But here's where faith and my work meet, and I want to be precise about it. I told [them] I'd like to test their AI. Not to catch them, because I want it to work. My kids are going to use tools like this. A builder telling me his AI tested itself is like a kid grading his own homework. So I write down what we expect before the test, anchor it publicly where it can't be changed, and publish what happens, even if I'm the one proven wrong. They're open to it. Nothing has run yet.

Faith says the future is possible. The test tells us which claims are true. I don't think you get to keep one without the other.

The film ends on a word: apocaloptimist. Someone who looks straight at how this could end and builds anyway.

Its last instruction is to upgrade ourselves, and I believe that more than anything I've written. But I want to say it carefully, because the way I first said it out loud was wrong. I said that if we upgrade ourselves, society will upgrade naturally. It won't. Not naturally. A better me doesn't make a data center pay its own bill. A better you doesn't make a lab open its books.

What the upgraded self does is go back into the cave and ask the questions, kindly, again and again, until the answers come with receipts. Upgrade yourself, then use that self to demand better from the systems. The inner work isn't a substitute for the outer work. It's what makes you trustworthy enough to do it.

A few days ago I wrote about a janky homemade Halloween cart and choosing presence over perfect. Same lesson, bigger stage. The mirror isn't only for me. When you watch someone look into it and not flinch, it gets a little easier to look into your own.

So the mirror is on. I've pointed it at the labs, and I'll keep pointing it there. But first I'm pointing it at me, as a man and as a father of a girl who is ten and won't be little much longer. There was a half-second on a street not long ago that I've been turning over ever since, and I'll tell you about it next time.

I don't know if we make it. Nobody in the film did either. But even the smallest spark, the smallest flame in the deepest dark, is still more powerful than the dark. That's not a guarantee. It's a place to start.
