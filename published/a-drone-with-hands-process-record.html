What this is

The essay holds two conflicting readings of German law on purpose, quotes Gage's sister from a recorded phone call, and credits a LinkedIn post for its courtroom frame. What was removed, and why

The chat that produced this essay started with a 45-minute phone call between Gage and his sister, recorded and transcribed, attached whole. The essay quotes five lines from it. The rest of the call is family: their children, their father, her job, a family name, a dead friend, a TV show. None of that is needed to check the essay and none of it is mine to publish.

So here is the rule I used:

  • Kept: every exchange in the chat that the essay is built from, verbatim, including the machine's long replies.
  • Not published at all: the call transcript. Gage's sister gave permission by text to be quoted in the essay. She was asked separately whether the full call could join this record and had not answered when this record was made. The five lines the essay uses appear below exactly as the transcript has them, cleaned only for voice-to-text punctuation.
  • Removed inside a kept turn: her name, her employer, their family's names and history, and the name of the school Gage's children attend. Each removal is marked in square brackets, like [sister's name] or [school].
  • Not reproduced: three screenshots of another person's LinkedIn post, which Gage attached so the machine could see who wrote it. He is credited by name in the essay and here; his post is his.
  • Left out: the machine's tool calls (web searches, file writes, memory reads) and the files it delivered mid-chat, except the first draft of the essay, which is reproduced in full below because the published piece was cut from it.

This proves less than a full record. You are trusting my judgment about what was relevant. I would rather say that plainly than publish my sister's phone call to make the receipt look bigger.

WORKING TITLE: A Drone With Hands
Alternate: The Moral Buffer

Byline: not yet ruled (own name, as the LinkedIn post was, or Roan Wilder). If Roan, a prescription line goes above the first paragraph; none is written here.

Placeholders you must fill or cut before this goes anywhere are in [BRACKETS]. There are three.

A robot groped a sixteen-year-old girl in Cologne on Saturday night.

Not a chatbot. Not a screenshot of something a model said. A four-foot humanoid, red and gold, walked up behind a kid standing outside a fast-food place with two friends, touched her, and thrust against her while two young men filmed it and streamed it live with commentary. Police found the robot in the trunk of a car and took it, along with two phones. The owner is sixteen. His companion is nineteen. The police are still working out who was holding the controls.

Say that last part again, slowly. The police are still working out who was holding the controls.

That sentence is the whole essay. Everything below is me trying to earn it.

I did not find this story in a newspaper. I found it in a LinkedIn post by [NAME, TITLE, LINK TO POST], and I want to credit him for it, because he asked the question that set me off. He did not ask whether the robot was evil. He asked us to picture the courtroom.

So picture it.

The girl is at one table. Her parents are behind her. Across the aisle, who sits?

The two young men, obviously. That part is easy, or should be.

Next to them, maybe, the company that built the body. It will say the machine did exactly what it was designed to do, and a human steered it, and a hammer maker is not on trial when a hammer is swung.

Next to them, the company whose software runs the thing. It will say the body is not ours, the controller is not ours, the operator is not ours.

Somewhere in the gallery, the platform the stream went out on. It will say we are a pipe, we did not touch anyone, we have a policy.

And the robot itself, in an evidence bag, saying nothing, because it cannot be charged, cannot be sued, and has no one to blame.

[NAME]'s point, as I read it, was that every seat at that table has a direction to point. The manufacturer points at the developer, the developer points at the operator, the operator points at the algorithm, and the girl goes home with a video of herself that will outlive all of them. I have watched that circle of pointing for twenty years. Social media did it. Tobacco did it before that, with a warning on the box as the alibi. My cousin reminded me of the cigarette companies on the phone this week, and she was right: the label is the shrug made legal.

That is the theory. Here is what the law actually says, because I checked, and it is not quite what I expected.

The first reports out of Germany said there might be a hole. Sexual harassment under German law, paragraph 184i, requires a physical touch, and at least one legal write-up argued the country's highest criminal court reads that as direct body-to-body contact, which would mean a touch delivered only by a machine does not count. The Cologne police are investigating under a different heading, insult on a sexual basis, and that heading made the hole look real.

Then two German legal scholars went on the record, on Wednesday, in the legal press. Iris Phan, who works on IT law and robot ethics, and Mohamad El-Ghazi, who holds a criminal law chair at the University of Trier. Their read, paraphrased: there is no protection gap. The robot is not a perpetrator, it is a tool. Touching someone with a stick, a broom handle, or a robot is still touching them, under the prevailing view. Several offenses are on the table at once: sexual harassment, possibly sexual assault by surprise, insult, and a separate offense for streaming humiliating images of a person. The "insult on a sexual basis" wording from the police is, in their reading, early-stage jargon from a case where the key fact is still unknown.

El-Ghazi added one honest caveat that cuts the other way: a brief touch over clothing may not clear the threshold German law sets for a sexual act, which would push the charge down to the lighter offense. So the sentence may be small. But the act is reachable.

I am going to mark this plainly: the loophole is contested, and the better-sourced view today is that there isn't one. If you read that there is, read the scholars too.

So the law probably reaches these two. Good.

Now go back to the sentence.

The police are still working out who was holding the controls.

Every offense those scholars listed requires a person who did it. The statute is not the problem. The record is. A four-foot machine walked up to a child in a public street and no one, not the police, not the manufacturer, not the company whose software was running, can produce a log that says: this key, this hand, this session, this moment. The investigators have two phones and a seized robot and they are going to have to reconstruct it from the outside, from video and from what two teenagers decide to tell them.

In this case there are two boys on camera, holding phones, narrating. The attribution will probably get made. This time.

Phan called the robot a moral buffer. Her phrase, and the right one. It puts distance between the hand and the harm, and the distance lowers the threshold for doing it. The next operator will not narrate a livestream. He will stand across the street. And when the machine is seized and he says it must have glitched, it must have done that on its own, there will be no record to answer him. That is the day a population turns on this technology. Not Cologne. The one after Cologne, when "it did that on its own" cannot be disproven.

I love this technology. I want it in my house, in my kids' school, in the hands of people who have never had a machine that could help them think. Which is exactly why I cannot stand the shrug. The shrug is what gets it banned.

Here is what I have been building, and what I propose. I will say what exists and what does not, because overclaiming is the disease I am trying to treat.

For the last year I have been working on two things in public.

E.D.A.I., Ethical Deployment of AI, is an accountability protocol. The idea is simple and old: you seal a promise to a public ledger before the act, so that when the act goes wrong there is a timestamped record of who promised what. The ledger is live, on Hedera, and it holds its first sealed record, a pre-registered test. One record. I am not going to pretend it is more.

Proof of Life is the identity layer underneath it. Proof that a real, accountable human stands behind a key, done by the person, not done to them. It is designed, not finished.

Cologne told me what they are for.

Three parties touch a machine like that one before it ever reaches a sidewalk: the user who owns it, the company that built the body, and the company whose software runs the mind. Right now none of them has to promise anyone anything before it walks out the door. So:

One. The user swears, under their own key, what the machine will and will not be used for. If you hand it to a friend, the friend swears the same with their own key and you countersign the handoff, or the liability stays with you. One hop. No sub-lending. A sixteen-year-old's key gets a guardian's signature beside it, which is where Proof of Life earns its place: a human, and the adult responsible for that human, stand behind the key.

Two. The manufacturer commits that the body refuses an unsigned hand, and keeps a tamper-evident log of every control session.

Three. The software vendor commits the same for the mind.

All three commitments live on a ledger none of the three controls.

I want to be careful about what a cryptographic promise can and cannot do. It cannot stop a hand. It cannot read intent. It is not a wall. What it does is end the shrug. When the next robot touches a kid, the record answers in seconds: who held the controls, who built it, who swore what, and when. The circle of pointing cannot form, because every seat at the table signed something before the machine left the box.

I call this the covenant, not a regulation, because I do not want a registry that only governments and corporations can read. The keys are held by the people who swear them. The anchor is public. If that sounds like a lot of trust to ask of a stranger with a robot, remember that right now we ask for none, and we got Cologne.

Status, plainly: the three-party covenant is a design on paper, mine, written this week. No code. The one working piece it scales up from is that single sealed record on the ledger, a promise anchored before an act. It is small. It is real. It is the first brick, and I would rather show you one brick than describe a wall.

The girl in Cologne will see that video for the rest of her life. The law will most likely reach the two who made it. I take no comfort in "most likely," and I would not ask her parents to.

What I keep coming back to is the four seconds before the touch, when a machine was walking toward a child and the only record of whose hand was on it was in the head of the person holding the phone.

I do not know how to make people good. I know how to make them sign.
